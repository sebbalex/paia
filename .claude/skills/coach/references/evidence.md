# Evidence base

What the coach's advice rests on. Each line is a claim, how strong the
evidence is, and where it comes from. Cite from here, by author and year, when
a recommendation needs its reason ("4×8 rather than 4×4: Seiler 2013"). Never
cite a study that is not in this file or in `section-11/SECTION_11.md`; if a
claim you want to make has no source here, say it is coaching practice, not
evidence.

Strength: **strong** = several trials or a meta-analysis agree; **moderate** =
one or two good trials, or consistent observational data; **weak** = thin,
mixed, or extrapolated from another sport or level; **practice** = what good
coaches do, without a trial behind it.

Most of these studies are on trained or elite athletes, often young men.
The owner is an amateur of 42 with ~6–9 h a week. Direction of effect transfers
well; exact magnitudes do not. Say so when it matters.

## Intensity distribution

- Successful endurance athletes do most of their volume easy (below the first
  threshold) and a minority hard. **Strong** (observational). Seiler &
  Kjerland 2006, Scand J Med Sci Sports.
- Polarised vs pyramidal: in trained athletes, polarised beat threshold-heavy
  training in some trials (Stöggl & Sperlich 2014, Front Physiol;
  meta-analysis Rosenblat et al. 2019, J Strength Cond Res), but the size of
  the edge is small and contested. Pyramidal distributions are what most
  successful athletes actually do across a season (Burnley, Bearden & Jones
  2022 vs Foster et al. 2022, point–counterpoint, Med Sci Sports Exerc).
  Pyramidal then polarised worked well in runners (Filipas et al. 2022, Scand
  J Med Sci Sports). **Moderate.** Consequence here: no forced 80/20;
  pyramidal is fine; what matters is that easy days are actually easy and
  hard days are actually hard.
- The "everything medium" week — every ride at IF 0.75–0.85 with no truly easy
  and no truly hard session — is the classic amateur pattern that the TID
  literature argues against. **Moderate** (inferred from the above).

## Interval formats

- 4×8 min at the highest sustainable intensity, 2 min recovery, beat 4×4 and
  4×16 for VO2max and power at lactate threshold over 7 weeks in recreational
  athletes. **Moderate.** Seiler et al. 2013, Scand J Med Sci Sports.
- Short intervals (30 s on / 15 s off, 3 sets of 13) beat effort-matched 4×5
  min in well-trained cyclists. **Moderate.** Rønnestad et al. 2015, Scand J
  Med Sci Sports; again in elite cyclists, Rønnestad et al. 2020, Scand J Med
  Sci Sports.
- 4×4 min at 90–95 % HRmax raises VO2max in moderately trained people.
  **Strong.** Helgerud et al. 2007, Med Sci Sports Exerc.
- Two high-intensity sessions a week is where most trials sit; more than that
  for an amateur with a job adds fatigue faster than fitness. **Practice**,
  consistent with the trials above.
- Sweet spot (≈ 84–97 % FTP) is time-efficient for raising sustainable power;
  there is **no** trial showing it beats a polarised or pyramidal mix.
  **Weak / practice.** Use it in base, don't sell it as proven.
- Block periodisation of high-intensity sessions (one week with 5 HIT
  sessions, then weeks of 1) beat the same sessions spread evenly in
  well-trained cyclists. **Moderate.** Rønnestad, Hansen & Ellefsen 2014,
  Scand J Med Sci Sports; review Issurin 2010, Sports Med. Only for a fresh
  athlete with a stable life; not a default for the owner.

## Durability

- Durability — how much power and HR drift after hours of work — separates
  riders of equal fresh FTP and is trainable. **Moderate.** Maunder et al.
  2021, Sports Med. For a long event such as a mountain marathon it matters more
  than a fresh 20-minute number.
- Aerobic decoupling (Pa:HR) under 5 % on a steady ride of an hour or more
  means the aerobic system held for that duration. **Practice** (Friel, The
  Cyclist's Training Bible). Only on steady rides: VI above ~1.05–1.1 makes
  the number describe the terrain, not the athlete.

## Strength training

- Heavy strength training (≈ 4–10 RM, 2×/week, 10–12 weeks) added to
  endurance training improves cycling economy and late-ride performance in
  trained cyclists, without weight gain that matters. **Strong.** Rønnestad,
  Hansen & Raastad 2010, Eur J Appl Physiol; Vikmoen et al. 2016, Scand J Med
  Sci Sports; review Rønnestad & Mujika 2014, Scand J Med Sci Sports.
- Once built, one heavy session a week maintains the gain in season.
  **Moderate.** Rønnestad, Hansen & Raastad 2010 (in-season maintenance), Eur
  J Appl Physiol.
- Concurrent training interferes with strength gains mostly through high
  endurance volume and running; cycling interferes least. **Moderate.**
  Wilson et al. 2012, J Strength Cond Res. Consequence: put the gym on a hard
  day or a rest day, never the day before the key session.
- For a man over 40, strength work also protects muscle mass and bone that
  cycling alone does not load. **Strong** (general exercise science; no single
  cycling trial).

## Masters athletes

- Endurance performance declines with age mainly through VO2max, and
  maintaining training intensity, not just volume, slows that decline.
  **Moderate.** Tanaka & Seals 2008, J Physiol. Consequence: keep some VO2
  work in every phase, even base.
- Recovery after hard sessions tends to be slower after 40; a 2:1 build:deload
  rhythm often suits better than 3:1. **Weak / practice.** Decide from their
  readiness data, not their age.

## Readiness and load monitoring

- ln(rMSSD) as a 7-day rolling mean against the athlete's own normal band is
  more stable than a single morning reading. **Strong.** Plews et al. 2013,
  Sports Med; Buchheit 2014, Front Physiol.
- HRV-guided training (hard on normal days, easy when HRV is suppressed) did
  at least as well as a fixed plan, often better, with fewer bad days.
  **Moderate.** Kiviniemi et al. 2007, Eur J Appl Physiol; Javaloyes et al.
  2019, Int J Sports Physiol Perform (road cyclists).
- ACWR as an injury predictor has fundamental statistical problems.
  **Strong** (methodological). Impellizzeri et al. 2020, Int J Sports Physiol
  Perform. Context only.
- Monotony (mean/SD of daily load) above ~2 with high load preceded illness
  in Foster's data. **Moderate.** Foster 1998, Med Sci Sports Exerc.
- Session RPE × minutes is a valid load for sessions with no power (gym).
  **Strong.** Foster et al. 2001, J Strength Cond Res.
- Overreaching vs overtraining: performance down plus mood and sleep
  disturbed for weeks is a medical question, not a training one. Meeusen et
  al. 2013, Med Sci Sports Exerc (ECSS/ACSM consensus).
- Sleep is the recovery input with the most leverage. **Moderate.** Halson
  2014, Sports Med.

## Fuelling

- Carbohydrate during exercise: none needed under ~1 h; 30–60 g/h for 1–2.5 h;
  up to 90 g/h of glucose+fructose for longer. **Strong.** Jeukendrup 2014,
  Sports Med; Thomas, Erdman & Burke 2016, ACSM position stand.
- The gut adapts: practising high intakes in training raises what is
  tolerated on race day. **Moderate.** Jeukendrup 2014.
- Protein about 1.6 g/kg/day maximises the gain from resistance training;
  more gives little extra. **Strong.** Morton et al. 2018, Br J Sports Med.
  At 75 kg that is roughly 120 g a day; use the weight in the brief.

## Tapering

- Best taper in the meta-analysis: about 2 weeks, volume cut 41–60 %,
  intensity and frequency kept. **Strong.** Bosquet et al. 2007, Med Sci
  Sports Exerc; Mujika & Padilla 2003, Med Sci Sports Exerc.

## Mountain biking

- Cross-country and marathon MTB are intermittent: long climbs near
  threshold, repeated short surges well above it, and technical descents that
  are not rest. Power output is more variable than on the road, and cadence
  on steep climbs lower. **Moderate.** Impellizzeri & Marcora 2007, Sports
  Med. Consequence for an MTB marathon goal: long climbing durability plus repeated
  surges, and time on the MTB for skill, not only road power.
